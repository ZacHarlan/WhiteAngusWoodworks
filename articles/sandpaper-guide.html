Sandpaper for Woodworking: The Definitive Grit Guide

Sanding is the bridge between building and finishing. It is the step that separates a professional-looking piece from something that looks like a weekend rush job. Get it right, and stain flows evenly into open grain, topcoats lay down glass-smooth, and the wood practically glows. Get it wrong, and every scratch, mill mark, and cross-grain gouge screams through the finish.

The uncomfortable truth is that more woodworking projects are ruined by poor sanding than by bad joinery. A tight dovetail under a botched finish still looks bad. A simple butt joint under a flawless finish looks intentional. That is how much sanding matters.

Yet sanding is the step most woodworkers want to rush. The cutting, shaping, and joinery are the exciting parts. Sanding is the work between the work. This guide is designed to make you as deliberate at the sanding bench as you are at the table saw.

What Is Sandpaper?

Sandpaper is an engineered cutting tool. It is not just rough paper—it is a precisely manufactured abrasive surface built from three components:

  • Abrasive particles: The mineral grains that do the actual cutting. Their size determines the grit number.
  • Backing material: The substrate that holds everything together—paper, cloth, polyester film, or foam. Heavier backings (C-weight, X-weight cloth) last longer and resist tearing on power sanders.
  • Bonding resin: The adhesive that locks the abrasive grains to the backing. Modern resin-over-resin bonds keep grains anchored under heat and pressure far better than the old hide-glue papers.

Common Abrasives Used in Woodworking

  • Aluminum oxide: The workhorse. Durable, self-sharpening (the grains fracture to expose fresh edges), and affordable. This is what you should be using 90% of the time on hardwoods and softwoods alike.
  • Garnet: A natural mineral that cuts softly and wears fast. It produces a finer scratch pattern than its grit number suggests, making it a good choice for final hand-sanding before stain. Less common now but still favored by hand-tool purists.
  • Silicon carbide: Harder and sharper than aluminum oxide, but more brittle. Primarily used for wet-sanding finishes between coats and for working on very hard materials. You will see it on dark gray or black sandpaper.
  • Ceramic: The most aggressive and longest-lasting abrasive available. Ceramic grains are self-sharpening and resist heat buildup. Excellent for belt sanders and aggressive stock removal. It costs more, but a single ceramic belt can outlast five aluminum oxide belts.

Understanding Sandpaper Grit

The grit number tells you the size of the abrasive particles. The system is simple: the higher the number, the smaller the particles—and the finer the scratch pattern. A 60-grit disc has large, aggressive grains. A 400-grit sheet feels almost smooth to the touch.

Here is how the grit ranges break down for woodworking:

  • Extra coarse (40–60 grit): Heavy stock removal, stripping old finishes, leveling badly warped surfaces. This grit removes wood fast and leaves deep scratches. Use it only when you have significant material to remove.
  • Coarse (80 grit): Flattening glue-ups, removing mill marks from rough-sawn lumber, initial shaping. This is where most furniture projects begin.
  • Medium (100–150 grit): The transition grits. These remove the scratch pattern from coarse sanding and bring the surface to a uniform texture. You will spend most of your sanding time in this range.
  • Fine (180–220 grit): Final sanding before finish. This is the sweet spot for preparing wood for stain or topcoat. Most woodworkers stop here.
  • Very fine (320–400+ grit): Sanding between finish coats, polishing final topcoats, and specialty finishing. Rarely used on bare wood.
Example sanding progression: For a walnut dining table, I start at 80 grit to flatten the glue joints, then progress through 120 → 150 → 180. I stop at 180 before staining. Each grit removes the scratch marks from the previous grit—that is the entire point of the progression.

How to Choose the Right Sandpaper

There is no single "best" grit. The right sandpaper depends entirely on what you are trying to accomplish in that moment.

Removing Mill Marks

Start at 80 grit. Planers and jointers leave subtle ridges across the board called mill marks. They are barely visible on raw wood but scream through a finish. An aggressive first pass at 80 grit levels these quickly.

Flattening Glue Joints

Start at 80 grit with a flat sanding block or random orbital. Dried glue squeeze-out sits slightly proud of the surface. Try to remove it with 150 grit and you will be there all day. Hit it with 80 first, then progress through your normal sequence.

Preparing Wood for Stain

Sand to 180 grit—and stop. Going finer (220+) on bare wood before staining actually polishes the pores closed. The stain cannot penetrate evenly, and you end up with a washed-out, blotchy mess. This is one of the most common mistakes I see.

Sanding Between Finish Coats

Use 320 grit between coats of polyurethane, varnish, or lacquer. The goal is not to remove material—it is to knock down dust nibs and create tooth (microscopic scratches) for the next coat to grip. A light scuff is all you need.

Sanding Tools Woodworkers Use

Sandpaper is only half the equation. The tool that holds it determines how effectively it cuts.

Hand Sanding Blocks

A flat cork or rubber block wrapped in sandpaper. This is the most controlled sanding method. Use it for final passes, detail work, edges, and anywhere you need to feel the surface through the paper. A flat block also prevents rounding over sharp edges that power sanders love to destroy.

Random Orbital Sanders

The most versatile power sander in the shop. The random orbital pattern (spinning and oscillating simultaneously) minimizes swirl marks. This is your primary tool for flat surfaces—tabletops, panels, and case sides. Start at 80 and work through 180 or 220.

Belt Sanders

Aggressive and fast. A belt sander removes material quickly, making it ideal for flattening large glue-ups, leveling uneven surfaces, and heavy stock removal. It requires a steady, experienced hand—a moment of inattention will gouge a hollow into your workpiece. Not recommended for beginners on finished panels.

Detail Sanders

The triangular-tip sander (sometimes called a mouse sander) gets into corners, tight joints, and routed profiles where a random orbital cannot reach. Essential for furniture with molding, inside corners, or spindle work.

Sanding Sponges

Foam-backed abrasive sponges conform to curves and contours. They are excellent for sanding rounded edges, routed profiles, and turned pieces. Available in multiple grits. I keep a few medium and fine sponges on the bench for quick touch-ups.

How to Sand Wood Properly

Good technique matters more than expensive tools. Here are the fundamentals:

Sand With the Grain

Always move the sandpaper in the direction of the wood grain. Cross-grain scratches are shallow cuts that refract light differently from the surrounding wood. They are invisible on raw wood and glaringly obvious under finish. This is the single most important sanding rule.

Control Your Pressure

Let the abrasive do the work. Pressing a random orbital into the surface does not make it cut faster—it slows the pad, generates heat, and creates uneven spots. The weight of the tool plus the weight of your hand resting on it is usually enough.

Follow a Proper Grit Progression

Never skip more than one grit step. A typical progression is 80 → 120 → 150 → 180. Each grit removes the scratch pattern of the previous grit. Skip a step and you are building your finish on top of scratches you cannot see—yet.

Avoid Swirl Marks

With a random orbital, keep the sander moving steadily across the surface. Do not let it dwell in one spot. Overlap your passes by about half. After power sanding, make a final pass by hand with the same grit, sanding with the grain, to erase any remaining orbital marks.

Remove Dust Between Grits

Vacuum the surface or wipe it with a tack cloth each time you change grits. Leftover coarse grit particles trapped under finer paper will scratch the surface and undo your progress. Dust collection at every step is not optional—it is part of the technique.

Common Sanding Mistakes

After years in the shop, I have seen every sanding mistake in the book. Here are the ones that cost the most time:

  • Skipping grits: Jumping from 80 to 220 because you are impatient. The 220 rides over the deep 80-grit valleys and never removes them. They reveal themselves the moment you apply finish.
  • Sanding across the grain: It takes one careless pass at 80 grit to leave a cross-grain scratch that requires starting over. Always check your grain direction before touching sandpaper to the surface.
  • Using worn sandpaper: Dull abrasive does not cut—it burnishes. Burnishing compresses wood fibers and seals the surface, preventing stain and finish from penetrating. If you are pressing harder to get results, it is time to change the paper.
  • Pressing too hard with power sanders: Excess pressure on a random orbital slows the pad rotation and creates pigtail scratches—small, curly marks that catch the light under finish. Let the weight of the machine do the work.
  • Sanding end grain the same as face grain: End grain absorbs stain differently. Sand end grain one grit finer than the face grain (e.g., face to 180, end grain to 220) to equalize stain absorption.

Sanding Before Finish

Your final grit depends on what finish you are applying:

Before Staining Wood

Sand to 180 grit. This leaves the pores open enough for even stain penetration without visible scratches. For blotch-prone species like pine or cherry, consider stopping at 150—the slightly rougher surface absorbs stain more uniformly.

Before Painting Wood

Sand to 150 grit. Paint is opaque and fills microscratches, so there is no reason to sand finer. You actually want a slightly rougher texture so the primer has mechanical grip. Degloss between paint coats with 220.

Before Clear Finishes (Polyurethane, Lacquer, Varnish)

Sand to 220 grit. Clear finishes are transparent—every scratch is magnified. The extra step from 180 to 220 takes five minutes on a tabletop and makes the difference between a good finish and a great one. Sand between coats with 320.

Pro Shop Tips

  • Use a pencil test: Scribble light pencil marks across the surface before sanding. When the marks are gone, you know you have sanded the entire surface evenly. This is especially useful on large panels.
  • Buy sandpaper in bulk: Changing paper frequently costs less than re-sanding a project because of dull abrasive. A box of 50 discs is far cheaper per disc than a 5-pack.
  • Keep a stiff brush handy: A brass-bristle brush cleans clogged sandpaper and extends its life by 30–50%. Brush across the paper, not along the rows.
  • Wet the wood to reveal scratches: Wipe the sanded surface with mineral spirits. It evaporates cleanly and temporarily shows you exactly what the finish will look like—including any scratches you missed.
  • Label your sanding discs: Once a disc is off the sander, you cannot tell 120 from 150 by feel. Mark the grit on the back with a permanent marker before loading.
  • Sand your glue squeeze-out, do not chisel it: A chisel can tear grain beneath dried glue. Let the squeeze-out cure fully, then flatten it at 80 grit.
  • Raise the grain before staining: Wipe the surface with a damp cloth after your final sanding. Let it dry, then lightly knock back the raised fibers with 220 grit. This prevents grain-raising from the stain itself and produces a smoother final result.
  • End grain needs extra attention: Sand end grain one grit finer than face grain to equalize stain absorption. End grain acts like a bundle of open straws and absorbs much more stain, making it appear darker if sanded to the same grit as the faces.

Frequently Asked Questions

What grit sandpaper should I use for wood before staining?

Sand to 180 grit for most stain applications. Going finer than 180 can actually close the wood pores and prevent even stain absorption. For lighter stains on open-grained woods like oak, 150 grit is often sufficient.

Can I skip grits when sanding wood?

Never skip more than one grit step. Each grit removes the scratch pattern of the previous grit. Jumping from 80 to 220 means the 220 paper is too fine to remove the deep 80-grit scratches, and they will show through your finish.

What is the best sandpaper for woodworking?

Aluminum oxide is the best general-purpose abrasive for woodworking. It is durable, self-sharpening, and works well on all domestic hardwoods and softwoods. For aggressive stock removal, ceramic abrasives outperform everything else.

Should I sand between coats of polyurethane?

Yes. Lightly sand between coats with 320 grit to de-nib dust bumps and create a mechanical bond for the next coat. Wipe with a tack cloth after sanding. Do not sand the final coat.

How do I know when to change sandpaper?

Change your sandpaper when it stops cutting and you start pressing harder, when abrasive particles are visibly worn smooth, or when the paper becomes clogged with dust. Worn sandpaper burnishes the wood surface instead of cutting, which can cause finish adhesion problems.
